# Vegetation & Habitat

Vegetation and habitat reflect the ecological condition of Louisiana's coast. Changes in salinity, water levels, flooding patterns, and land loss can influence which plant communities persist, the habitats available to fish and wildlife, and the overall condition of coastal wetlands.

The results in this section examine future ecological conditions through marsh classification, vegetation communities, habitat suitability and diversity, and wetland value assessments to understand the composition and quality of these systems.

For the 2029 Coastal Master Plan, these results help provide context beyond land area alone. They can help identify where ecological functions may be maintained, where habitat conditions may decline, and where future restoration opportunities could support important coastal resources.

This analysis shows projected marsh types across coastal Louisiana.

Marshes are classified based on environmental conditions that influence wetland vegetation, including factors such as salinity and hydrology. The results show where fresh, intermediate, brackish, and saline marsh conditions may occur in the future and can help identify areas where changing environmental conditions may alter the character and presence of coastal wetlands over time.

[Dig Deeper info button text: The ICM models annual land cover, as represented by the relative abundance of vegetation species for areas of vegetated wetlands and the amount of bareground. The model utilizes mean salinity during the growing season (May through August), the maximum 2-week salinity, mean water level, and variability of the water surface to determine how vegetation changes over time in response to changing environmental conditions. For most species, mean salinity during the growing season and variability of the water surface are the main factors driving changes in vegetation. The coverages of individual species then determine habitat type (e.g., bottomland hardwood forest, fresh to saline marsh) so that changes in the distribution and abundance of those different habitat types can be tracked over time.]

This analysis shows projected vegetation communities across coastal wetlands.

The results are based on modeled responses of wetland plant species to changing environmental conditions such as salinity, water levels, and flooding patterns. By tracking changes in the mix of species that make up vegetation communities through time, the analysis provides insight into how ecological conditions may shift across the coast and where different habitat types may expand, contract, or transition.

This analysis measures the variety and relative abundance of habitat types within an area.

The metric is based on the number of habitat types present and how evenly they are distributed across a definedn area. Areas with a greater mix of habitat types generally support a wider range of ecological functions and species. These results help identify places where habitat diversity may increase, decrease, or remain relatively stable under future conditions. Greater habitat diversity is generally considered a positive indicator of estuarine system health.

This analysis evaluates wetland quality using measures of habitat suitability and ecological condition using methods and classifications similar, but not identical, to the Wetland Value Assessments developed through the Coastal Wetlands Planning, Protection, and Restoration Act (CWPPRA) program.

The assessment combines information about vegetation, hydrology, land-water configuration, and other environmental factors to estimate how well wetlands support different habitat types. Unlike land area metrics, which focus on the quantity of wetlands present, ICM Wetland Value Assessment results provide information about wetland condition and ecological function. These results can help identify areas where habitat quality may improve or decline over time. This set of metrics is especially useful for looking at indicators of swamp health that are not well represented in other FWOA metrics.
