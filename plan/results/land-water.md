# Land & Water

The balance between land and water is the foundation of Louisiana's coastal landscape. It can impact communities, habitat, navigation, fisheries, and many of the ecological and economic functions that support coastal communities.

The results in this section show how land and water conditions may change under a FWOA over the next 50 years. They include projections of expected land and water distribution and changes to land and water conditions projected under the two project selection scenarios used for the 2029 Coastal Master Plan. The results help describe both the amount of land that may remain and the condition of that landscape.

Because reducing land loss is one of the primary goals of the Coastal Master Plan, understanding where land is expected to persist, where loss is most likely, and how landscape conditions may change is an important part of identifying future coastal challenges and evaluating potential restoration opportunities.

This analysis shows the expected distribution of land and water across the coast based on a range of future sea level rise projections.

This analysis shows projected land and water conditions under the two project selection scenarios used to evaluate restoration projects for the 2029 Coastal Master Plan.

Each scenario represents a different set of future environmental conditions and produces a different projection of the coastal landscape over time. These results help illustrate the range of future land and water conditions considered during project evaluation and provide context for understanding how future land loss may vary under different assumptions about environmental change.

This analysis shows projected land types and water depths across the coast over time.

The results combine information about land cover and water depth to provide a more detailed picture of future landscape conditions. Land areas are classified into categories such as vegetated wetlands, uplands, and unvegetated areas, while water areas are grouped by depth. These data can help identify changes in wetland condition, transitions between landscape types, and shifts in shallow and deep-water environments that may not be apparent from tracking land loss alone. While Louisiana’s coastal ecosystem depends on a healthy mix of land types and water depths, there is no shortage of deep water in this region. Generally, shallow water is more conducive to maintaining nearby wetland health and integrity than deep water is. Additionally, many restoration and risk reduction project types cannot usually be built cost effectively in deep water.
